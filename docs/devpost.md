# Footnotes (Devpost write-up)

> A clinical walking aid turned into a living piece of music that grows when you walk in time
> with it.

## Inspiration

For people with Parkinson's, walking can lose its rhythm: steps get shorter and uneven, and
sometimes the feet feel glued to the floor ("freezing of gait"). One of the better-studied
helps is surprisingly simple: walk to a steady beat. This is Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation
(RAS); a 2022 meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials found it improved stride length and gait
speed and reduced freezing. But the usual cue is a metronome, and a metronome is boring. We
wanted to keep the steady beat that does the work and make it something you'd _want_ to walk
to. DurHack's theme was Composition, so we let the walker compose the music.

## What it does

- **A phone in your pocket** feels your steps and sends them to the laptop.
- **20 seconds of normal walking** sets the beat just above your natural pace.
- **The beat always leads and never chases you.** What changes is the music on top: walk in
  time and shakers, bass, piano, melody and sparkle join in, one per bar; drift and it strips
  back to a clear pulse that's easy to find again.
- **If a freeze is detected**, every layer fades and a calm, recognisable recovery tune plays
  on the same beat; when walking resumes, the music rebuilds.
- **A live visual score** on the laptop draws the walk and the music together: each
  instrument as its own lane drawn from the notes actually played, and each step as a dot
  placed by its timing against the beat.
- **A patient app on the phone** designed for Parkinson's: huge, tremor-proof buttons,
  read-aloud on every screen, press-and-hold anywhere to stop, and each walk drawn as a little
  piece of music afterwards.

## How we built it

- **Web app** (Vite, React, TypeScript) for both screens; a small **Node WebSocket relay**
  pairs a phone and a laptop by a 4-letter room code; a Cloudflare tunnel gives the phone the
  HTTPS it needs for motion sensors.
- **Step detection** on the phone: acceleration magnitude, gravity removed, low-pass
  filtered, adaptive threshold and peak prominence, the filter's delay measured and removed,
  and a rhythm-aware second look that catches the faint step of the leg without the phone.
- **Clock sync**: NTP-style pings; the median offset of the fastest exchanges. Measured error
  about 1 ms through the tunnel, so steps can be compared with the beat to the millisecond.
- **"In step" score**: each step's phase against the nearest beat, then circular statistics
  (the mean resultant length R) over the last 8 steps, a tempo-match factor, and smoothing.
- **Music**: fully synthesised with Tone.js in D major (Dmaj7, Bm7, Gmaj7, A7sus4); levels
  balanced by measuring each layer offline; layers enter and leave on bar lines with fades,
  with hysteresis so the arrangement never flickers.
- **Freeze detection**: the freeze index (power at 3–8 Hz ÷ power at 0.5–3 Hz, Moore et al.
  2008), one shared implementation used by our evaluation, the replay and the live watch.
- **Evaluation** on the Daphnet dataset (10 patients, 237 annotated freezes), with
  leave-one-patient-out thresholds; real recordings replay through the live pipeline in the
  demo.

## Challenges we ran into

- **The beat that felt twice too fast.** Slow walking (about one step a second, common in
  Parkinson's) looked identical to fast one-legged strides, and our automatic guess doubled
  the pace. We stopped guessing and made "every bump is a step" the default.
- **The missing foot.** A phone in one pocket feels the far leg faintly; every other step went
  missing. Rather than lower the threshold everywhere, the detector now looks back between
  steps that are twice the usual gap apart for the faint bump in the middle. Simulated catch
  rate went from about 55% to 99%.
- **Connections that died silently** when the phone locked. Keepalives, a watchdog and
  reconnect-on-return fixed it.
- **Honest freeze numbers.** It's easy to report a high detection rate. Ours is high too, but
  with far too many false alarms for daily life, and we say so.

## Accomplishments that we're proud of

- The beat is rock steady (spread of beat gaps under a millisecond), and the music genuinely
  sounds like music, not a metronome with beeps.
- Phone-to-laptop timing good to about a millisecond over the internet.
- A freeze detector evaluated fairly on real patient data, with every number generated by the
  same code the app runs.
- A patient app built around tremor and low vision from the start.

## What we learned

- Signal processing on real bodies is messy: our simulated walker was easy, real pockets
  weren't.
- Musical rules (bar lines, fades, hysteresis) matter as much as the maths for making
  adaptive music feel calm.
- Evaluating fairly (leave one patient out, count false alarms) changes the story, and makes
  it more believable.

## What's next for Footnotes

- Co-design with people with Parkinson's and physiotherapists.
- Music on the phone with wired headphones, for real walks outside.
- An ankle-worn sensor option for better freeze detection.
- A proper clinical evaluation.
